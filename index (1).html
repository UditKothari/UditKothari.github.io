Learning-based battery dispatch

Can a battery controller learn when to charge and discharge in a microgrid where solar output and EV demand keep changing?

The research gap

In our literature review, several learning-based dispatch studies tested decisions on simplified energy models. I wanted to know whether those same decisions would hold up in a detailed electrical model where the battery inverter has to respond to changes in load and keep the microgrid stable.

What I’ve done so far

I helped shape the research question and co-led the model development and feasibility testing. We started with a reference model in Simulink and Simscape Electrical, then brought the solar, battery and load models together. I helped plan the tests around specific operating conditions so we could tell where a problem came from and rerun the same case after a change.

We started with load steps, then tested changing sunlight, excess solar and a daily profile with EV charging. Working through those cases made it clear that when the microgrid disconnects from the grid, the battery already has a job: keeping voltage and frequency steady. That changed what we plan to let the learning controller adjust in islanded operation.

What the simulations showed

  1. Changes in demandThe battery picked up the load when demand rose.
  2. Surplus solarVoltage started swinging when the battery switched to charging. We isolated the problem to the battery’s voltage controller and reran the test after retuning it. The simulated microgrid stayed stable during charging, and the discharge check still passed.
  3. Daily profileThe model followed a compressed daily pattern of solar and EV demand. We used that run to check how the microgrid responded when loads changed over the day. The controller comparison comes next.

RMIT FMM Lab · Research internship

Wearable biosensor hardware

I designed a flexible circuit board for a glucose and sodium sensor that had to fit inside the arm of a pair of glasses.

Learning the sensing problem

Biochemistry was new to me. I learnt enough about how each sensor produced a signal and how the two could interfere to make informed circuit and layout decisions. I worked between my biochemistry and electrical supervisors, checking what the sensing sites needed with one and what the circuit could reliably deliver with the other.

Going back to the circuit

I used op-amp fundamentals to work through how to condition the small sensor signals, then checked the power supply around the Bluetooth bursts. My calculations pointed to a supply dip with the original coin cell, so I moved to a LiPo and gave the analogue and digital sections their own regulators. Diffusion calculations led me to separate the two sensing sites on a T-shaped flex.

What changed between revisions

Sensor interference
Separated the sensing sites on the flex after checking diffusion.
Supply dip
Reworked the battery and regulator layout around the Bluetooth load.
Parts went out of stock
Checked replacement parts against the original circuit requirements and repeated the relevant simulations before updating the layout.

Going through several revisions taught me where to spend time. I checked the calculation or simulation most likely to force a redesign before committing hours to layout. I took the revised flex PCB through fabrication.

EV detection from meter data

I built classifiers to infer EV ownership from 48-interval household load profiles. I looked at overnight ramps and stretches of high demand, then checked precision and recall on held-out households to see how often other appliances were mistaken for charging.
